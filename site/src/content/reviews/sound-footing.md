There is a particular quiet to a theatre that is poor but not neglected. Neglect has a smell — mildew, cold ash, the faint sweetness of rot in the wings. The Zarya Palace of Culture has none of it. Raised in the years when such palaces were raised for towns that have since slipped off the better maps, it is cold the way a cathedral is cold, and kept the way a good knife is kept: not gleaming, but sharp where it counts. The radiators clank in a language of their own. The gilt in the lobby has gone the color of weak tea. And the floor — I will come to the floor, because the floor is the reason one travels four days to a town one cannot pronounce on the first attempt.

I was met by a member of the maintenance crew, a man who introduced himself by his patronymic and his trade in the same breath, as though they were one fact. He did not ask whether I had come to review the company. He asked whether I had come to see the stage. This, I would learn, is the order of priorities here, and after an hour in his company I had stopped finding it strange.

The stage at Zaryansk is wood. Bare, finished Siberian larch — *listvennitsa* — the slow, tight-ringed timber that grows a single grudging millimeter a year through winters that would kill a softer tree, and is hard for it, and resinous, and full of the small dark knots that are the wood's memory of those winters. It is, by every contemporary standard, the wrong floor. The dancing world settled that argument two generations ago. Somewhere in the long thaw of the 1960s the great vinyl houses arrived with their rolls of grey marley, and a touring company could at last carry its own reliable surface into any strange theatre and lay down certainty over the unknown. The surface a dancer meets today is almost always that — a skin of vinyl, the same in Tokyo as in Toronto, forgiving and predictable and faintly anonymous.

The dancers know it, and they complain. I have heard the complaints in three languages now and they do not vary: the bare wood is uneven, it catches the shoe where you do not expect, it is slick where the resin has risen and grabs where a knot breaks the grain. A floor that argues back. You cannot trust it the way you trust the vinyl, and a dancer at full speed lives or dies by what can be trusted underfoot.

The crew has heard the complaints too, for longer than I have been alive, and they are unmoved. Their position — delivered to me beside the prompt corner, with the patience of men explaining water to a fish — is that the floor is not supposed to be trusted. A surface that catches teaches the foot to be honest. A floor that punishes a lazy landing produces, over years, a dancer who does not land lazily. They believe, in short, that the floor breeds soundness, and that the soft grey certainties of the vinyl era have raised a generation of dancers who are faster, lovelier, and built like glass.

I should confess here to an inherited weakness. I come from a line of essayists; my great-grandfather could begin a paragraph with a swallow and end it with the meaning of a life, and I have never been entirely cured of the habit. So permit me a digression, because the floor sent me to it.

The turf this season has belonged almost entirely to the get of Rudolf — that brilliant, brittle sire whose sons and daughters run as though the ground were an insult to be left behind as quickly as possible. They are magnificent. They are also, with a regularity that has stopped being remarked upon, breaking down: a sesamoid here, a condyle there, careers measured in months. We have bred the horse for one virtue, pure speed, and quietly traded away the older virtues — the dense bone, the sound foot, the constitution that lets an animal simply keep going — as though sturdiness were a tax on brilliance rather than its foundation. Watching the Zaryansk crew oil their unforgiving floor, I thought: here is a stable that has decided, against the whole drift of the age, to breed for bone. (The irony they would not enjoy, so I will keep it to these pages: larch is precisely the wood from which one builds a velodrome track. The sturdy timber is also the fast one. The categories were never as opposed as we pretend.)

And beneath all this argument, holding it up, is the strangest thing in the building.

A floor like this must be sprung, or it would shatter the dancers it claims to strengthen. The crew could not buy a sprung subfloor; there is no money, and no money has been the condition here for so long it has become a kind of architecture in itself. So they improvised. Some years ago, an hour's drive into the taiga, the army abandoned an installation — a vast steel lattice that had stood through the cold decades listening, they say, for the incoming signal, for the small bright dot that would mean the end of the world. The world declined to end. The lattice was left to the weather. And the crew, who waste nothing, went out and brought back its springs: the great industrial coils on which the antenna's mass had been suspended, so that the earth's own tremors would not blur what it strained to hear. Those springs are now under the stage. The mechanism built to feel for annihilation has been repurposed to cushion the landing of a girl in the second act.

I drove out to see it, of course. A reviewer should review what moves him, and the installation moved me more than I expected to admit. It was never meant to be looked at — it was meant to look, or rather to listen — and so it has all the unselfconscious authority of the genuinely functional. Against the flat white of the taiga it reads as an accidental Tatlin, a Constructivist monument that no committee approved and no sculptor signed: rust laid over the steel in long oxblood streaks like a deliberate patina, the geometry of the array softened now by birch saplings pushing up through the platform, the whole thing humming very faintly in the wind in a register you feel in the teeth rather than hear. It is better than most of the public sculpture I have been paid to admire this year. I award it, with full seriousness and no authority whatsoever, my warmest notice of the season. The state built it to detect the future. It has aged into the past, and become beautiful on the way, which is more than the future ever managed.

Tomorrow the company dances. I have not yet seen them take a single step, and so I will say nothing about whether the crew's hard faith is rewarded in the bodies it has shaped — whether these are sound dancers or merely stubborn ones, whether a floor can in fact raise a spine. That is next week's column, and it would be dishonest to write it before the curtain.

For tonight it is enough to report that in a town the maps have given up on, a handful of men keep a wooden floor alive on a bed of doomsday springs, in the belief that the right kind of difficulty makes a dancer last. I came four days to disagree with them. I find, on the eve of the performance, that I have not yet decided to.

— *R. A. P.*
